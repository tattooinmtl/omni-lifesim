What happened

How generations work
  • Time: one fly day lasts 20 seconds at 1× speed. Egg 1 day, larva 4 days, pupa 4 days, so an egg becomes an adult in about 9 days, as in real Drosophila at 25 °C. Adults live about 40 days.
  • Genes: every fly carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent: sensitivity of each smell receptor, learning rate, escape threshold, flight speed, lifespan, eggs per clutch, and body colour. Its Kenyon-cell wiring is inherited too. Each copy has a 5% chance to mutate. Learned memories are not inherited.
  • Mating: a mature, receptive female picks the least related free male (by kinship from the family tree). After mating she carries a clutch of eggs and, like a real fly after sex peptide, rejects males for 1.5 days.
  • Eggs: she flies to rotting fruit and lays them. Each fruit holds 6 young at most, and the garden holds 20 adults; clover nectar is the only real food, so a crowded garden goes hungry.
  • Inbreeding: children of relatives have a higher inbreeding coefficient, which shortens their life and shrinks their clutches.
  • Death comes in many ways. The risk of dying of old age rises steeply with age (a Gompertz curve, as in real flies), sooner for inbred flies. Two days with an empty stomach kills. Birds swoop by day and kill 85% of the flies they catch, and 35% even of those that jump away, so a quick Giant Fiber escape (a heritable threshold) improves the odds without guaranteeing them. Spider webs hang at flying height near the clover; a stuck fly has a few seconds to tear free, which strong, well-fed flies do more often. Cold nights and rainstorms make flying expensive, and heavy rain knocks flies down and drowns weak ones.
  • Food is the bottleneck. Only one fly can feed on a clover at a time, and nectar refills slowly, so flies compete. Larvae eat the rotting fruit; when a fruit is eaten bare, the larvae left on it starve, and the fruit slowly rots back.
How the brain works, and what's real

Each fly runs about 60 rate neurons named after real Drosophila cell types, wired in the textbook layout:

  • Antennae: olfactory receptor neurons on the left and right (Or42b for esters, Or56a for geosmin) feed projection neurons.
  • Lateral horn: innate valence. Esters attract, geosmin repels.
  • Mushroom body: 30 Kenyon cells keep a sparse code of the current smell. Sugar fires PAM dopamine neurons, bitter or a hit fires PPL1. Dopamine weakens the Kenyon-cell synapses onto the matching output neuron, which is how real flies learn smells.
  • Vision: LPLC2 looming detectors drive the Giant Fiber, which triggers a fast escape jump. LC10 spots the other fly.
  • Descending neurons turn left or right, fly forward, land, and extend the proboscis to feed.

The brain view in the corner uses real anatomy: the JRC2018Unisex template brain and 13 of its neuropil regions, downloaded from Virtual Fly Brain and simplified to fit the page. Each region glows with the simulated neurons that live there: antennal lobes with each antenna, lateral horn with innate valence, mushroom body calyx and lobes with Kenyon cells, optic lobes with the looming and fly detectors, and the gnathal ganglia with taste and feeding. Dopamine shows on the mushroom-body lobes, PAM in green on the horizontal lobes and PPL1 in red on the vertical lobes, matching where those neurons project in real flies.

The wiring is still a teaching-scale circuit, not the full connectome. The real FlyWire brain has about 140,000 neurons.
